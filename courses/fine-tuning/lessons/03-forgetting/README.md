# 03 - Catastrophic forgetting

## Concept

Training on new data pulls the weights toward the new data, and nothing in the loss tells them to stay where they were.
A model that is fine-tuned long enough, or with a large enough step, **forgets** what it knew: **catastrophic
forgetting**. You saw the symptom in lesson 02: every fine-tuned row had a worse loss on the Holmes text than the base.

You cannot see forgetting unless you measure it, and "worse loss on old text" is the cheapest honest measure: hold out
text of the original kind, evaluate before and after, and report the **relative damage**, `(after - before) / before`.
The new-style numbers alone would hide it.

Three mitigations, from cheapest to most work:

1. **Smaller steps, fewer steps.** Lower learning rate and early stopping move the weights less (lesson 02's table).
   They protect the old behaviour by also limiting how much the new one can be learned.
2. **Replay (data mixing).** Put a share of the *old* kind of data into every batch. The gradient then keeps pushing the
   weights toward both distributions at once, so neither is abandoned. It costs compute (the old windows are steps not
   spent on the new data) and needs old data (or a stand-in).
3. **Change fewer parameters.** Freeze most of the model (lesson 04), or add a small adapter and leave the original
   weights untouched (lessons 08 and on). Forgetting is limited by construction when the old weights cannot move.

The right amount of replay is a trade-off, not a number to look up: more replay means less forgetting but a smaller gain
on the new target at a fixed number of steps. This lesson makes the trade-off a table you can read.

## On a real project

Replay data is usually a sample of the original pre-training or instruction mix, sometimes regenerated by the base model
itself ("self-distillation") when the original data is unavailable. Regularisers that pull weights toward their old
values (elastic weight consolidation) exist but are rarely used for language models; replay and parameter-efficient
methods do most of the work in practice.
